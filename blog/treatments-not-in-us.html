Korean Skin Treatments Not Available in the US: A Clear Guide

Updated October 2026  ·  6 min read

If you've been researching Korean skin care, you've probably noticed the same names keep appearing: Rejuran, Juvelook, exosome therapy, Innotox. They are widely used in Korean dermatology clinics, but they are not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration, so you won't find them at clinics in the United States. This guide explains what each treatment is, how it is regulated, and what to ask a licensed dermatologist before deciding whether any of them is right for you.

1. Rejuran Healer (PDRN Injection)

Approved in Korea

What it is

Rejuran is a polynucleotide (PN) injection derived from salmon DNA, part of the family of treatments often labelled PDRN. It is designed to support the skin's own repair processes. Unlike fillers that add volume, Rejuran aims to improve the quality of the skin itself, such as texture, elasticity, fine lines and hydration. It's considered a foundational treatment in Korean dermatology, often described as "skin rehabilitation" rather than cosmetic correction.

How it is regulated

Rejuran is approved by Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and has been widely used there since 2014. Polynucleotide injectables have not been approved by the FDA, so they are not offered by US clinics. Because Rejuran is made from salmon DNA, tell your doctor about any fish allergy.

What to expect

Clinics usually suggest 2 to 3 sessions, 3 to 4 weeks apart. Any change is gradual rather than immediate, and results vary from person to person. Small raised bumps at the injection points usually settle within a day or so.

2. Juvelook: Next-Generation Skin Booster

Approved in Korea

What it is

Juvelook is a Korean-developed injectable that combines hyaluronic acid with poly-D,L-lactic acid (PDLLA) microspheres. The HA provides immediate hydration while the PDLLA microspheres dissolve slowly over months, continuously stimulating new collagen. Most standard fillers do one or the other. Juvelook is designed to do both at once, for overall skin quality rather than targeted volume correction.

How it is regulated

Juvelook is approved by Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and CE-marked in Europe. It is not FDA-approved and is not currently offered in North America.

What to expect

Some hydration is visible soon after treatment, while collagen stimulation develops gradually over 3 to 6 months. Results vary, and your doctor will advise on the number of sessions.

3. Exosome Therapy

Used in Korean clinics

What it is

Exosomes are nano-sized vesicles packed with growth factors, peptides, and cell-signalling proteins. In Korean clinics, exosome serums are often applied to the skin straight after microneedling or laser treatments, with the aim of supporting healing and calming redness.

How it is regulated

No exosome product is FDA-approved, and the FDA treats applying them after microneedling as using an unapproved drug. Evidence is still developing and products vary, so ask your doctor what the product is, where it comes from, and what it is expected to do.

What to expect

Usually offered as an add-on to another treatment on the same day. How much difference it makes varies, and your doctor can tell you whether it suits your skin.

4. Innotox: Liquid Botulinum Toxin

Approved in Korea

What it is

Innotox is a liquid form of botulinum toxin type A made by the Korean company Medytox. Conventional toxins such as Botox and Dysport arrive as a freeze-dried powder that a nurse mixes with saline right before your appointment. Innotox comes pre-mixed in a standardized solution, which practitioners like for consistent dosing from one vial to the next. It is used the same way as any type A toxin, softening forehead lines, frown lines, and crow's feet, along with jaw slimming and the light micro-dosing technique often called skin botox that is popular in Seoul.

How it is regulated

Innotox has been approved by Korea's Ministry of Food and Drug Safety since 2013. It has not been submitted to the FDA, so US clinics use other type A toxins such as Botox, Dysport, Xeomin, Jeuveau and Daxxify instead.

What to expect

Effects typically begin within a few days, peak at about two weeks and last roughly three to four months, similar to other type A toxins.

5. Before You Consider Any of These

Treatments that are not approved where you live deserve extra care. A few things to do first:

  • See a board-certified dermatologist, who assesses your skin in person and recommends what suits you.
  • Ask what product will be used, where it comes from, and what the evidence shows.
  • Share your medical history, allergies and any previous treatments.
  • Plan recovery time before you fly home, and get travel insurance that covers medical treatment.
  • Remember that your doctor at home may not be familiar with these treatments if you need follow-up care.
